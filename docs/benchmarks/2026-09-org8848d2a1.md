## Disposable-table write smoke benchmark

Scenario: 30 rows per transport, using a uniquely named custom standard table.
The harness assigned row IDs in advance, verified row counts after create, update,
and delete phases, and removed the table after the run.

|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| POST | Individual | 1 | 30 | 2.322 s | 12.92 |
| POST | Individual | 8 | 30 | 0.668 s | 44.93 |
| POST | Individual | 32 | 30 | 0.286 s | 104.74 |
| POST | Batch(30) | 1 | 30 | 0.509 s | 58.95 |
| POST | CreateMultiple(30) | 1 | 30 | 1.550 s | 19.35 |
| PATCH | Individual | 1 | 30 | 2.700 s | 11.11 |
| PATCH | Individual | 8 | 30 | 0.684 s | 43.88 |
| PATCH | Individual | 32 | 30 | 0.481 s | 62.35 |
| PATCH | Batch(30) | 1 | 30 | 0.804 s | 37.29 |
| PATCH | UpdateMultiple(30) | 1 | 30 | 0.760 s | 39.48 |
| DELETE | Individual | 1 | 30 | 4.827 s | 6.21 |
| DELETE | Individual | 8 | 30 | 0.980 s | 30.60 |
| DELETE | Individual | 32 | 30 | 0.786 s | 38.17 |
| DELETE | Batch(30) | 1 | 30 | 2.488 s | 12.06 |
| DELETE | Individual | 20 | 30 | 0.769 s | 38.99 |

This smoke run confirms that GET results cannot be generalized to writes. Parallel
individual requests were fastest at this payload size. POST batching was faster
than individual POST at DOP 8 but slower than DOP 32. PATCH batching and
UpdateMultiple were similar. Transactional batch deletion was substantially slower
than parallel individual deletion, and DELETE appeared to plateau around DOP 20.

The CreateMultiple result should not be read as evidence against the bulk API. This
was its first invocation against a newly provisioned table, the fixed scenario order
can introduce warm-up bias, and 30 tiny rows do not amortize endpoint overhead. Use
repeated, warmed runs with larger payloads before choosing batch or bulk defaults.
The current general default ceiling of 20 remains reasonable: it nearly matched DOP
32 for DELETE in this run while leaving the server's lower `x-ms-dop-hint` in control.

### Methodology correction for the next run

The original `Count 30` and `BatchSize 30` settings produced exactly one `$batch`
HTTP envelope. Its requested DOP of 1 therefore did not compare sequential and
parallel batch envelopes. The revised harness uses independent `BatchMaxDop` and
`BulkMaxDop` matrices, reports the number of envelopes, warms all transports,
randomizes scenario order within each verb, repeats each sample, and can report
median throughput. A subsequent run should use `Count` greater than both `BatchSize`
and `BulkSize` so that envelope-level concurrency can actually occur.

## Warmed concurrent-envelope benchmark

Scenario: 100 rows per transport, three randomized measured iterations after
warm-up. Individual requests used DOP 1, 8, 20, and 32. `$batch` used five
20-operation envelopes with requested DOP 1, 4, and 8; because only five envelopes
exist, the last case has an effective concurrency ceiling of 5. Bulk operations
used two 50-row envelopes with requested DOP 1 and 4, making the latter's effective

### Conclusions

- The individual-request default ceiling of 20 is supported by PATCH and DELETE,
  where DOP 32 provided no median gain. POST at DOP 32 had a higher median but a
  much lower minimum, while DOP 20 was stable.
- Five concurrent 20-operation batch envelopes produced the best POST median
  (165.52 ops/s), about 2.9 times the individual DOP 20 result.
- Two concurrent 50-row bulk envelopes produced the best PATCH median
  (131.85 ops/s), about 2.6 times the individual DOP 20 result.
- Concurrent batch DELETE and individual DELETE converged around 33–35 ops/s.
  Batch remains valuable when each change set's atomicity is required, but separate
  envelopes have no global ordering guarantee.
- These are useful starting points for similarly small records, not universal
  defaults. Plug-ins, payload size, alternate keys, relationships, service-protection
  limits, and transactional requirements can change both throughput and correctness.
